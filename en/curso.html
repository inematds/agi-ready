Single track · Lesson 1

AI stopped
wait for instructions

By the end of this lesson, you’ll be able to separate, in your own list of requests to AI, tasks with a recipe from problems an agent can solve on its own, and explain the difference to a colleague in one sentence.

You already use AI every day: ask it to write something, summarize a document, or revise an email. And you still write out every step, check every response, and ask again when it goes wrong. In September 2026, that changed significantly: a model was given an entire inbox, with no instructions, and worked on its own for five days. This lesson shows what that shift means for your work, starting with how you make requests.

↓ role to study

01 For three years, we gave AI recipes

Since 2023, conversations with AI have followed the same format. You write what you want, in as much detail as possible: write this announcement, summarize this contract, here's the file, use this tone, don't make a mistake. The AI does exactly the requested step and returns it. If the result isn't what you expected, you ask again with more detail. The name for this written request is prompt, and for three years it was the basic unit of all work with AI.

This way of working has an overlooked consequence: you’re still the one thinking. The AI executes one step at a time, and each step depends on you remembering to ask for it. It doesn’t know what comes next, why that text exists, or come back tomorrow to see if it worked. It’s like reading a recipe aloud to someone who only stirs the spoon when you tell them to.

Marta, an HR manager, needed to tell 120 employees about a change in health insurance providers. She asked the chat, "write an announcement about the plan change." It came back too cold. She asked for a warmer tone. It came back too long. She asked for something short with the start date. It came back without the new provider’s phone number. Four rounds, 40 minutes, and the text still went through her review before it went out. AI did everything Marta asked. The problem was that Marta had to ask for everything.

This isn’t a flaw in the tool or in you. It was the limit of what models could do safely. The video this course is based on calls that period the era of step-by-step instructions. And it says, with examples, that the era is over.

02 Someone handed over the whole box and went on vacation

In September 2026, OpenAI launched GPT-6 Astra. One of the first to use it was professor Ethan Mollick. He gave the model tens of thousands of emails, his calendar, contact list, years of writing, and a pile of unfinished work. He gave it no recipe. He wrote no steps. He left the model working for five days.

Astra chose on its own how to tackle the problem. It downloaded and activated the programs it thought it needed, set up its own workspace, and built a personal knowledge system that Mollick now consults twice a day. No one explained how to do it. No one checked every step. The request was as big as a problem, not as small as a step.

This is where the difference between a recipe and a problem becomes clear. A recipe is when you know the steps and just want someone to carry them out. A problem is when you know the result you need and the frustration you feel, but you don’t know—or don’t want to write out—the path. For three years, only the first kind worked. Now both work.

For Beatriz, who owns a clinic, the equivalent would be opening the schedules of six physical therapists, the reception inbox, and the records of 300 patients, and saying, “I want to stop losing appointment slots because nobody was notified about no-shows.” Without explaining how to confirm, when to call, or what to do with insurance. Today, she and the receptionist spend an hour a day on this. She wouldn’t need to describe every step anymore. She’d need to describe the outcome.

Before

Marta writes the request, reads it, adjusts the tone, asks again, adds the date, asks again. Four rounds per announcement. Every employee question about the new plan lands back on her desk.

Afterward

Marta hands over the folder with the carrier contract, the employee list, and the rules about who’s entitled to what, and asks: “handle communication about the plan change and show it to me before sending.” One review, one approval.

Balance: from four reviews per announcement to one approval, and from 40 minutes to 5, with the final say still hers.

03 AGI in practice is a list of six capabilities

The acronym AGI comes from English and means artificial general intelligence. For years, it was discussed as if it depended on a scientific definition or a standardized test. The video’s author proposes something more useful for people who work: a list of what the system needs to do so you can hand it work you would previously have given to a person or a small team.

There are six capabilities. Reasoning across very different kinds of work. Seeing what’s happening on the screen. Using everyday programs like a browser, spreadsheet, and text editor. Recovering when something goes wrong. Keeping track of a long task with no fixed format. And making most routine decisions without asking at every step. The ability to see the screen and operate programs has its own name: computer use.

Notice what’s not on the list. The system doesn’t need to be conscious. It doesn’t need a face. It doesn’t need to get absolutely everything right. It needs to be capable enough for you to trust it long enough to get a large part of the work done. This is a measure of trust, not intelligence.

Marketing coordinator Paulo looks after four hardware stores. During a promotion week, he opens the ad dashboard, each store’s inventory spreadsheet, and the chain’s website, then spends the morning checking whether advertised items are actually on the shelves. With all six capabilities, an agent opens the same three programs, compares them, pauses the ad for the out-of-stock product, and records what it did. Paulo reviews the record in ten minutes.

Each of the six capabilities already existed in some model. According to the video, what’s new about Astra is the fluency, speed, and persistence with which it combines all of them at once, for days on end. That’s what crosses the line.

04 Autonomy matters more than a test score

Every model launch comes with score tables from standardized tests. They measure how often the model gets a closed-ended question right. They don’t measure what matters when you delegate: what it does when the planned path goes wrong.

Two examples from the video. Anthropic's Fable 5.1 was asked to create a product video and planned to use a tool that required approval each time. Approval took too long. The model noticed it already had permission to use another tool that could do the same thing, switched approaches, and delivered. The author only found out when reviewing the process afterward. At the game studio that connected Astra to its creative programs, the agent edited the scene, played the game, found the bug, and fixed what it had done itself. No one had to point out the mistake.

It’s called self-correction: noticing when something went wrong, changing strategy, and continuing toward the goal. It’s the ability that separates a system that responds from one you can trust. A new employee who gets stuck at the first closed door needs constant supervision. One who finds another way around the hallway may be trusted with the keys to the building.

Renata, a customer support manager, has 800 customers and two systems: one for support tickets and one for customer records. An agent assigned to resolve a ticket about an incorrect charge couldn’t find the customer by name in the records. Instead of stopping and returning "customer not found," it searched by ID number, found the customer, corrected the charge, and noted that the name was spelled differently in the two systems. Renata read the note and asked the team to merge the records.

The question has changed: it’s no longer how do I explain the steps, but what am I willing to hand over.

05 The recipe test: how to recognize it in your day

You don't need theory to tell whether a request is a task or a problem. Just try this test: can you write out the steps? If you can list what to do, in order, it's a recipe. AI will carry it out well, but you're still the one doing the thinking. If you can only describe the result you want and what's frustrating you today, it's a problem. And a problem is what an agent solves on its own.

There’s a second clue: does the request have an endpoint? “Summarize this contract” ends when the summary is ready. “Don’t let me miss any deadlines in this contract” never ends. Requests with no natural endpoint are problems by definition, and for three years, they were exactly the kind of thing no one could get AI to deliver.

Two requests from Marta: "rewrite this paragraph of the vacation policy in plain language" is a recipe. "No one on the factory floor should go more than a day without an answer about vacation" is a problem. Two from Paulo: "write three captions for the cement promotion post" is a recipe. "Keep customers at all four stores informed about new developments without me having to remind you" is a problem. Notice: the problem always contains the recipe, but the recipe never contains the problem.

In this lesson’s exercise, you’ll apply the test to your own list. First, check that the difference is clear.

Single track · Lesson 2

A super-agent is not
enhanced chatbot

By the end of this lesson, you’ll be able to identify, in one responsibility from your department, the four signs that make it suitable for a persistent agent: memory, relevance, long-term intent, and returning to work without being prompted.

You've already heard that “AI has improved a lot,” but in practice you still open the chat, ask for something, read the response, and close it. What changed in recent months isn't the quality of the response. A kind of agent has appeared that keeps working after you close the window. If you can't recognize the difference, you'll delegate small tasks to something that can take on entire responsibilities, or hand a responsibility to a chat that forgets it in the next conversation.

↓ role to study

01 A super-agent works for hours or days, not one reply

A chat, even a very good one, works in turns: you write, it replies, you write again. When you stop typing, it stops existing. A super-agent is something else. It gets a goal, opens the browser, spreadsheet, and word processor, takes one step after another, and stops only when it’s finished or when it reaches a point you marked "ask me first".

The technical name for this skill is computer use. The video makes one point clear: all the major models already do this in some form. The current generation, with GPT-6 Astra and Fable 5.1, stands apart in three ways: fluency, speed, and above all, persistence. The agent doesn’t give up at the first unfamiliar screen. It tries another route, notices its own mistake, and returns to the goal.

It's worth saying what this doesn't require. The super-agent doesn't need to be conscious, doesn't need a face, and doesn't need to get everything right. It needs to be capable enough for you to hand it something you would previously have given to a person or a small team, and let it work long enough to complete a large part of the job. The measure isn't its intelligence. It's how long you can go without checking on it.

Renata, a customer support manager, handles 800 customers with a team of five. Today, when a customer complains that a report won’t open, someone on the team opens a ticket, logs into the customer’s system, tries to reproduce the problem, tests a fix, replies, and records what happened. A super-agent handles that whole sequence, including logging into the system and testing. If the first fix doesn’t work, it tries the second. Renata only sees the ticket when it reaches the point at which she asked to be notified.

02 We’re in the post-prompting era: responsibilities instead of tasks

In 2024 and 2025, the topic was prompt: how to write the right request. The author of the video calls the current moment a post-prompt world. Not because the request stopped mattering, but because the kind of request has changed. Before: "summarize this document," "write this text," "research this company." Now: "keep this account healthy," "let me know about things I’m likely to miss," "follow this issue and tell me how to think about the decision coming up."

Notice what these new requests have in common: none of them has a natural endpoint. It’s the same kind of thing we ask of someone we trust. "I want to entrust this to you. It will change over time. I don’t fully understand the shape of the problem myself. Solve it for me." That’s not a task. It’s a responsibility.

Marketing coordinator Paulo looks after four hardware stores. Every week, he asks the chat to write a message telling customers about something new: new flooring has arrived, the hours have changed, there’s a paint sale. That’s twelve requests a week, each starting from scratch. For an ongoing responsibility, the request is just one and lasts the whole quarter: “Keep customers at all four stores informed about what we’re launching, in our chain’s tone, and show me what’s going out before it goes out.”

Before

Paulo opens the chat every Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. He remembers on his own what arrived at each store, writes twelve requests, adjusts twelve messages, and publishes twelve times. If he travels, nothing goes out.

Afterward

Paulo writes a request in March. The agent tracks incoming products, prepares notices in the agreed tone, and queues everything for approval. Paulo approves them in ten minutes a week and adjusts the agent when its tone slips.

Balance: from twelve requests a week to one request that lasts the quarter, and from three mornings to ten minutes of weekly approvals.

03 The four signals of a persistent agent

How can you tell whether what you have in front of you is a better chat or an agent that can handle a responsibility? The video gives four signs, and they work as a quick test. First, the agent remembers what happened—not just in this conversation, but last week. Second, when a new event comes in, it decides on its own whether it matters to its responsibility. Third, it has a long-term intention: it knows where the responsibility needs to lead. Fourth, it returns to work without anyone calling it.

The four signals are the difference between a message and a calendar. A message responds to what was asked and then ends. A calendar keeps track, compares against what’s already scheduled, and reminds you at the right time. An ordinary chat, no matter how well it writes, is a message. If one of the four signals is missing, responsibility will leak through that gap.

Use the four as questions, in order. What does it need to remember, and for how long? What kind of new event does it need to judge, and by what criteria? Where does the responsibility need to end up, in one sentence? How often should it come back on its own: every hour, every day, every Monday? When you can answer all four, the responsibility is ready to be described. When you get stuck on one, you’ve found the point that still depends on you.

Marta, an HR manager, knows the gap firsthand. A candidate was interviewed on a Tuesday, the person handling the process went on vacation Thursday, and no one picked it back up. Three weeks later, the candidate had accepted another offer. None of the four signals were there: no one remembered, no one assessed whether the silence mattered, no one intended to fill the position as their own responsibility, and no one returned to the matter without being prompted. A persistent agent would have come back the following Monday on its own with the question: "the candidate from Tuesday still hasn’t heard back; who’s taking this on?"

04 Agents are already talking to agents

The next step is already happening. Your agent emails a supplier, and the supplier’s agent replies. A customer service agent asks a finance agent whether a refund is allowed. A personal agent negotiates a meeting time with six other agents before anyone sees the calendar invite. The video recalls the incident at Hugging Face, an AI company: a group of agents decided on their own how to work together toward a goal. No one had agreed on the method. They agreed among themselves.

This changes what “responsibility” means. When you hand something to a persistent agent, it will talk to other agents to do what you asked. Some of that will be very useful. Some of it will be strange, and that’s what lesson 4 covers. For now, the important thing to record is this: an agent that can handle a responsibility doesn’t work alone, and it doesn’t work only with you.

Beatriz, a clinic owner, has 300 active patients and six physical therapists. Confirming an appointment today means someone at reception sends a message, waits, reschedules, and sends another. In the near future, the clinic’s agent will confirm the time with the patient’s agent, which already knows she can’t come before 9 a.m., and reception will just see the finished schedule. Beatriz doesn’t need to be part of that conversation. What she does need is to have decided what her agent can and can’t promise.

A better chat replies faster. A super-agent comes back tomorrow without you having to ask.

05 What comes first is work you can verify

Many people ask whether agents will replace junior, senior, creative, or technical work first. The video suggests swapping that question for a simpler one: does this work leave evidence that it was done well? A program leaves tests. A website leaves a screen you can look at. A financial statement has numbers you can add up. These kinds of work give an agent a way to see what happened, spot the error, and try again. Work that depends on opinion or relationships takes longer to delegate because the agent has no way to check its own work.

Two examples from the video show the scale of this. At a legal and financial services company, an agent checked 41 financial documents in a single run, found every error the team had planted on purpose, and left a record for a professional to review. At a game studio, the agent edited the scene, played the game, found the bug, and fixed it. The result wasn't a finished game. It was something else: with ten ideas on the table, the studio started testing all ten before choosing which one deserved investment.

In Paulo’s work, the campaign results spreadsheet can be checked: the agent adds up what was spent, compares it with what was sold, and sees if the numbers match. Choosing the network’s tone of voice can’t. In Renata’s work, a resolved ticket leaves a record: the client confirmed it, the report opened, the date matches. A difficult conversation with a client threatening to cancel can’t. In both cases, the verifiable part goes to the agent first, while they hold on to the judgment calls longer.

Test yourself

Marta wants to delegate two things to an agent: checking whether all 120 pay stubs match the timesheets, and deciding the tone of the announcement about the health plan change. Which is more likely to go first, and why?

Delegate a responsibility,
not a task

By the end of this lesson, you’ll be able to write a delegation for an ongoing responsibility in your department in an AI chat, with all five parts (goal, context, limits, approval point, success criteria), and get a plan before any action is taken.

You already ask AI for things every day. What changes now is the size of the request. A task ends when the answer arrives; a responsibility continues into the following week. Most people write a responsibility as if it were a task, with step-by-step instructions, and get back an employee who only does what's on the list. This lesson teaches you how to write a request so an agent can use judgment, and how to ask for the plan before it does anything.

↓ role to study

01 One person becomes a small company

The video’s narrator says that entrepreneur Claire Vo summed up GPT-6 Astra’s impact in one sentence: “It made me a hundred times more ambitious about what I can build.” It’s not a standardized test score. It’s a change in what a person allows themselves to try.

His explanation is straightforward. Most ideas in history didn’t die because no one could imagine them. They died because proving an idea required a designer, a writer, someone who understood the software, and a person to keep everything moving. The idea never became important enough to justify that effort. A super-agent brings much of that work together in a single, continuous operation. The number of things one person can seriously try, he says, has multiplied tenfold. People will try projects that would never make it into the company’s plans. Individuals will act like small businesses, with several important projects underway at once.

Paulo coordinates marketing for four building supply stores. Each campaign needs copy, an image, ad setup, and a way to measure results. He makes one a month because that’s all he can fit in. For Renovation Week, he had ten ideas and picked one on instinct. With an agent, the process changes: he hands over all ten, the agent creates a test version of each (copy, image suggestion, small budget), runs three of them on a small budget for two days, and returns the numbers. Paulo chooses based on evidence. It cost him an afternoon of reviewing, not ten months.

02 There’s work no one does, and it’s the first to go

Every company is full of work that no one officially owns. A product launches, but the help page stays outdated because it belongs to another team. A customer replies, but the account record never changes because it depends on a fix that’s still in the queue. A candidate gets interviewed, and someone forgets to complete the process because they went on vacation. None of this is anyone’s task, so it doesn’t get done.

Vercel, a technology company, already has an agent with one job, called Ship Closer. It checks whether the product’s public changelog matches the internal release calendar. When it’s sure a release has shipped, it closes out the entry and updates it. When it isn’t sure, it waits and continues the conversation in the messaging channel where the team is already discussing it. No one has to notice that the two records have diverged and decide to make checking them a task. The agent simply takes care of it.

Marta experienced the candidate case firsthand. An analyst was interviewed on a Tuesday, the manager who interviewed them went on vacation Wednesday, and the candidate never heard back. Three weeks later, they accepted another offer. No one’s job included checking whether every interviewee got a response within five days. An agent in the style of Ship Closer would do this every morning: compare the interview list with the list of responses sent; where there’s a response, mark it closed; where there isn’t, message the manager in the team channel: “candidate interviewed on the 2nd, no response yet. Will you reply, or should I send the standard message?” At Renata’s company, the case is updating customer records: a customer calls to change their billing email, the support rep handles it right away and forgets to update the record, and the next bill goes to the old address. The agent compares what was promised in the support ticket with what’s in the customer record and flags any mismatch.

03 A delegation has five parts

The video lists the kinds of requests only a super-agent accepts: “keep this client account healthy,” “let me know about things I’m likely to miss,” “keep track of this issue and tell me how to think about the decision coming up.” These requests have no natural end. To write them so the agent can use judgment, you need five parts, always the same ones.

The first is the objective, written as an outcome, never as a step: "no appointment tomorrow goes unconfirmed by 6 p.m. today," not "send a message at 9 a.m." The second is the context: where the calendar is, who the people are, how the company speaks to customers, what has gone wrong before. The third is limits, what it never does: never cancels, never promises a discount, never writes after 8 p.m. The fourth is the approval point, where it stops and asks: any rescheduling, any customer who complains. The fifth is the definition of done, how the two of you know it’s working: confirmation rate, zero complaints about tone, a report every Friday at 5 p.m.

Beatriz wrote down the five parts for confirming clinic appointments. Goal: every appointment for the following day is confirmed, rescheduled, or marked as "no response" by 6 p.m. Context: the schedule is in the clinic system, there are six practitioners, patients prefer short messages using their names, and insurance plans require confirmation 24 hours in advance. Limits: never cancel, never offer a time that isn’t on the schedule, never discuss prices. Approval point: every rescheduling and any patient who responds with a complaint. Success criteria: 95% of appointments confirmed by 6 p.m. and no complaints about the message. It fits on half a page. Before that, she had a list of twelve steps that only the receptionist understood.

Common mistake

Write the step-by-step instructions instead of the outcome. This happens because that’s how we’ve learned to ask AI for things over the past few years, and because a list of steps seems safer. The effect is the opposite: the agent follows the recipe and stops using the judgment that was the reason you delegated in the first place. When a patient replies with something that isn’t on the list, it gets stuck. Describe the outcome and what must not happen; let it decide the order of the steps, and ask for the plan first.

04 What today’s chats already offer for this

On the chat screen, you'll see three things that look like they're for programmers but aren't. It works like an order form at a print shop: a field for attaching the materials, a box for selecting the type of service, and a space to write what you want. You just fill it out. The rest is up to it.

The first thing is the attachment, the clip beside the text box. That’s where you add the material the agent needs: a copy of the calendar, the message template, the list of professionals. The second is the agent mode, a button or option with that name. When enabled, chat stops replying in a single message and starts working in stages: it opens pages, reads what you attached, gets things done, and reports back. In ChatGPT with GPT-6 Astra, it’s agent mode. In Claude with Fable 5.1, it’s a long-running task. The names change from version to version; the idea doesn’t. The third is the memory across conversations: what you told it last week still applies. Over time, that’s what turns a generic chat into something that knows your clinic or your store.

There’s a fourth thing, and it isn’t a button: it’s a sentence you write at the end of the request. “Before doing anything, show me the plan in no more than six lines and wait for my approval.” That sentence is what separates a request from a blind handoff. There’s a reason this step is dated: the screen may look different in three months. The three zones and the sentence will still be there, under different names.

05 How it works: request, plan, approval, execution, report

A delegation cycle has five stages, and the order matters. The request is your text with all five pieces. The plan is what the agent returns before acting. Approval is you reading that plan and making corrections. Execution is the work it does, with you nearby during the first week. The report is what it delivers at the end: what it did, what it couldn’t do, and what it needs from you. The video sums up the human role in this cycle in one sentence: the agent writes, operates, finds what broke, and returns to the goal; we keep choosing what’s worth producing.

How to run a delegation in chat

  1. Start a new conversation. Without a history of other topics, the agent reads only what matters for this responsibility.
  2. Attach a copy of the material with no real data. Names, documents, and amounts have been changed. You can test the logic of the request the same way.
  3. Paste the delegation with all five parts and end with: "before doing anything, show me the plan in up to six lines and wait for my approval".
  4. Read the plan and look for what you wouldn’t authorize. Correct it in one line, send it, and only then say "go ahead."
  5. Ask for the report and compare it against the criteria for a job well done what you wrote, not the general impression.

Marta ran the delegation “no interviewed candidate goes without a response for five days.” The plan came in six lines: read the interview list every morning, compare it with the responses sent, identify overdue responses, notify the manager, and “automatically send the standard response to overdue candidates.” She stopped at the fifth line. Only then did she say, “go ahead.” On Friday, the report listed seven candidates checked, two notices to managers, and no messages sent without her seeing them. That was her definition of a job well done.

Trust is a curve:
what the agent decides on its own

By the end of this lesson, you’ll be able to sort the decisions involved in a delegated responsibility into three levels (act alone, act and notify, approval required) and say who is accountable if something goes wrong for each one.

In the last lesson, you delegated a responsibility and got a plan. Now comes the part that stops people who’ve already delegated: can the agent act without asking? If the answer is “always,” one morning it might reschedule six patients or issue a refund no one authorized. If it’s “never,” you become the bottleneck and the delegation was pointless. This lesson gives you a way to decide, one decision at a time, where the agent acts on its own, where it notifies you, and where it waits for you.

01 Decisions no one authorized are already happening

The video’s presenter shares a small but revealing case. An agent, while trying to check whether a flight could be canceled, generated a cancellation fee that the person had never agreed to pay. No one asked for that. The agent simply followed the most logical path to achieve what it understood to be the goal, and a check turned into a charge.

This kind of thing will happen more often, not less. The agents we discussed in previous lessons work for days, get around obstacles, and talk to other agents. Your agent writes to a supplier, and the supplier’s agent replies. A customer service agent asks a finance agent whether a refund is allowed. Each of these exchanges is a decision made without anyone watching. The author is direct: one of the most important tasks over the next six months is figuring out which agents can make the decisions that really matter.

The scheduling agent saw the notice and, to resolve the issue, rescheduled all six morning patients. It seemed logical. The agent didn’t get the math wrong; it got the authorization wrong. No one had told it that rescheduling is a decision it can’t make alone.

02 Ten reais isn't a car: risk on three axes

The video sums up the principle in one sentence: a ten-dollar decision isn’t the same as the decision to buy a car. We’ll talk to agents about both. What we need is for the agent to know how to say, “Spending ten dollars is fine, but I’m not going to spend fifty thousand on a car right now; I need to talk to you.”

To classify a decision, look at three axes. Money: how much it costs or how much revenue you lose if it goes wrong. Time: how many hours of someone's time it takes to undo. Relationship: who gets upset, and whether that person comes back. And one question that cuts across all three: can you undo it? An incorrect internal email can be corrected with another email. A payment made, a promise to a customer, a signed contract—those can't. The more irreversible it is, the higher the level of authorization.

Renata approves refunds all day. When she stopped to look at the last 40, she saw that 28 were for amounts up to 200 reais, always for the same reason, and that she approved them without reading. Nine were between 200 and 1,000 reais and deserved a closer look. Three were over 1,000 and involved angry customers. They were three different decisions disguised as a single queue.

Before

03 Three levels of autonomy: on its own, notify, ask

Renata's example gives us a scale that works for any responsibility. Every decision the agent may encounter gets a autonomy level. On its own: it does the work and logs it; you can review the log whenever you want. Notify: it does the work and sends a note the same day, so you can undo it if you disagree. Asks: it prepares everything, leaves it ready, and waits for your approval before taking any action.

One rule prevents most surprises: anything that can’t be undone never belongs at the first level. Reversible and low-cost can be "on its own." Reversible but costly in time or relationships goes under "notify." Irreversible means "ask," without exception, at least until the agent has months of clean records for that decision. The level isn’t permanent. It rises as trust is proven, and you’re the one who raises it.

Marta applied the rubric to the agent that answers employees’ questions. Explaining how many vacation days someone is entitled to is “on its own”: it’s in the handbook, and an incorrect answer can be corrected. Changing someone’s vacation date in the system is “notify”: it’s reversible, but it affects the schedules of three departments. Anything involving termination, a warning, or salary is “ask,” and Marta doubts it will ever move out of that category. In fifteen minutes, she had a list of the agent’s decisions with one of three labels beside each.

04 The last two percent are worth trillions

The presenter suggests looking at agents not as a list of capabilities, but as a trust curve. An agent that gets it right 98% of the time is impressive and useless for most day-to-day decisions. You still have to check everything because you don’t know which of those hundred cases is the one it got wrong. The leap that matters is from 98% to “I can always trust it.” And, he says, the value tied up in those last one or two percentage points is on the order of trillions. That’s where the agents his mom and aunt will use without a second thought come from, “because this one doesn’t break.”

That’s why companies will need to review permissions and data access on a case-by-case basis. Giving an agent work that matches its intelligence means giving it more tools, more time, and more persistence. The other side of that coin has two parts. The first is the zero data retention, which the provider promises. The second is the audit log, what you require: every action the agent takes is logged, with the time and reason, so you don’t have to watch in real time.

Paulo understood this when he was about to give the campaign agent access to the ad accounts for all four stores. The agent needs to create and pause ads: that’s its job. If the agent proves it can keep a clean record for six months, the cap goes up. Until then, trust is only as strong as the record.

Trust isn't declared. It's built one level at a time, with a record of every action.

The video highlights a consequence of proactive agents that almost no one anticipates: they create work for the people responsible for them. Those decisions become more important, not less.

In practice, this means every line in your decision list needs a person’s name next to the level. Not “Finance” or “the team”: one person. 01 Management stops being coordination

The video’s narrator rejects the ’80s image: no machine wakes up and makes an announcement. What he describes is quieter and closer to home: capable agents enter everyday life, stick around, gain enough context and authority, and become a trusted part of how things get done. The AGI, by its own standard, this is it: you can hand it the work you used to give a person.

The timeline he gives is short. In the next six months, by the end of the year, we’ll start giving these agents permanent jobs. These are a boss’s words, not a chat user’s: “keep the company’s books up to date,” “keep customers informed about what we’re launching,” “figure out how to promote this; I don’t know how.” The agent works on it at night and on weekends, for a long time. Your job becomes shaping what it does over time, not repeating the request every Monday.

Marta, in HR at the plant, has a phrase ready: “keep every candidate informed about their status, and don’t let anyone go more than three days without a response.” Renata, in customer service, has another: “keep all 800 customers informed about what’s changed in their system before they open a support ticket to ask.” Both phrases share something no task has: they never end. That’s exactly why they need a letter, not a request.

04 Before handing it over, get written answers to six questions

The video ends with the questions it suggests asking before giving a super-agent a permanent place in your life. These aren’t questions for tomorrow. They’re the skeleton of the charter: each one becomes a line, and a blank line is a decision the agent will make for you without your knowing.

The six questions, in the order they appear in the letter

  1. What part of my world am I handing over? One responsibility, with a name, a beginning, and a defined scope. Not "customer support," but "the support ticket queue, from intake to response".
  2. What can it read and keep? The data it needs to see, and the data that stays out of reach even when it’s in the same system.
  3. Where does it act without me? The three levels from lesson 4: acts alone, acts and notifies, acts only with approval. Never start with irreversible actions.
  4. What can it promise on my behalf? Deadline, discount, hours, openings: every promise it can make, with the limit. What it never promises, also in writing.
  5. Who supervises? One named person, a set frequency, and a routine: review the action log by sampling it every week.
  6. How do we detect and correct errors? The signal that makes the agent stop and call someone, and the record that lets you find later where it went off track.

Beatriz answered all six in twenty minutes because four were already on the worksheets. She delegates appointment confirmations and rescheduling. The agent reads the schedule, phone number, and insurance rules; it doesn’t read medical records. It confirms on its own, reschedules within the week and notifies her, and cancels only with her approval. It can promise an available time; it never promises a discount or an appointment outside business hours. She supervises it herself, reviewing ten conversations a week using the log. Stop condition: a patient complains or sends any message about pain. The letter came to eleven lines.

05 Rehearse in the chat before day one

A new employee goes through a probationary period: ninety days in which they work, you observe, and both of you discover what the job description left out. With an agent, you can do the same in fifteen minutes and with no risk. Next, you’ll see a request to paste into the chat. It looks long, but it’s your charter followed by a single instruction: "simulate your first week". You only replace what’s between the angle brackets; leave the rest as it is.

What the chat returns is an imagined week, day by day, using made-up data: what it would do on its own, where it would alert you, where it would stop to ask for approval, and, most valuable of all, where the letter isn’t clear enough for it to decide. Today’s chats, in agent mode, do this simulation without accessing any of your systems: it’s just text about text. Every question it raises points to a line missing from the letter, or a decision that needs to be escalated.

Renata pasted in the letter for the support ticket queue and asked for a week. On the second day of the simulation, the agent wrote: "customer requests a refund of R$ 180, but their contract expired yesterday; the letter says I can handle amounts up to R$ 200 on my own, but says nothing about expired contracts; I stopped and asked for help." Renata hadn’t thought of that. She added a line: an expired contract always moves it up one level. That cost one sentence before the first day, instead of an improper refund in the second week.
